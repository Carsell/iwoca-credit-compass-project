## Data quality

- 400 duplicate event_id — deduplicated on load
- 18 businesses missing region — grouped as 'Unknown'
- 12 credit scores outside 300-850 — set to null, not clipped, because a clipped 1200 becomes a plausible 850 and stops looking like an error
- 1 businesses created after the first observed month — flagged, tenure not computed for them

## Results

1. **Sector matters, but less than the spread within a sector.** Mean credit score runs from 565 (Retail) to 648 (Healthcare), a gap of 84 points. Within a single sector the standard deviation is 70 points, so sector alone is a weak basis for a decision about any individual business.

2. **Engagement and credit score correlate at r = 0.79 across businesses, and at a median of r = 0.02 within them.** Pooling the data suggests that using the product raises your score. Following each business separately, that mostly disappears. The pooled number is largely telling us that healthier businesses both engage more and score higher, which is a fact about who signs up, not about what the product does. Establishing a product effect would need businesses compared against similar businesses that did not engage.

3. **Churn shows up as a level, not as a drop, and that changes what you would do about it.** 13% of businesses left during the window. Across their active months they averaged 9.5 engagement events a month against 10.3 for businesses that stayed, a gap of 0.8. But comparing each churner's final three months against its own earlier baseline, the average change was -0.4 events and only 55% of them declined at all — about what you would expect from chance. So the businesses that left were mostly quiet from the beginning. They did not go quiet. An alert built on a sudden fall in usage would fire late and mostly on the wrong accounts; screening the book on sustained low usage would find them, and could be run once a quarter rather than monthly.

4. **Approval is driven by score, and applications by engagement.** 1,433 applications were made and 45% were approved. Approval runs from 2% in the 300-500 band to 84% above 700. Applications came almost entirely from businesses that had used the product that month, so the funnel narrows at engagement before it narrows at credit.

5. **The partner funnel loses most of its customers before the credit decision.** Of 19,000 started applications, 3,383 (17.8%) end in an accepted offer. The costliest step is **completed**, where 8,322 customers are lost, more than any other. The steepest percentage drop is elsewhere, at **accepted** (55% carry through), which is worth separating: a bad rate late in the funnel affects fewer people than a mediocre rate at the top. Effort goes where the customers are.

6. **The pre-qualification test looks conclusive and is not.** Pooling every application, the treated arm completed at 50.4% against 59.1%, a lift of -8.7 percentage points with a 95% interval of ±1.5pp. That is the number a standard A/B calculator returns, and it is wrong, because the feature was switched on per partner and not per customer. There are six independent units in this experiment, not 19,000. Comparing partner-level conversion rates instead gives a lift of -8.6pp with an interval of ±7.4pp, which does not exclude zero. The draw explains most of it: the treated arm averaged 0.39 on partner traffic quality against 0.63 for control, so the arms were not comparable before the feature was switched on. With six partners, randomisation will not fix that. The true effect built into this simulation is +2.5pp, so the pooled analysis does not merely overstate its precision, it gets the sign wrong and reports it with a ±1.5pp interval. The honest read is that this design cannot resolve an effect of the size we care about. The fix is more partners, randomisation within partner, or a difference-in-differences against each partner's own pre-period. Not more customers.
